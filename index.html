Evidence summary · Overall verdict

一句話總結

4+2R 不是偽科學騙局，而是把數個「有部分證據」的真實原理由具醫學背景者重新包裝。但它把「腸道菌因果」與「重設代謝定點、永不復胖」當作核心賣點而誇大；其實際療效很可能來自熱量限制與高蛋白等通用機制。最關鍵的證據缺口：查無任何針對「4+2R」命名方案本身的同儕審查 RCT 或前瞻性試驗。

主張一：腸道菌相 證據薄弱

「肥胖源於腸道菌相失衡；養好菌就會瘦、治代謝病」

Evidence for causality between gut microbiota dysbiosis and obesity in humans remains correlative rather than definitively causal. While observational studies show associations between specific microbial profiles and obesity, human RCTs of microbiota-targeted interventions demonstrate modest and inconsistent metabolic improvements with minimal weight loss effects.

Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) Trials

Weight Loss Effects:

Meta-analyses of FMT RCTs show no significant weight reduction compared to placebo. A 2023 meta-analysis of 9 RCTs (303 participants) found no significant difference in weight loss between FMT and placebo groups.[1] A 2023 systematic review of 10 RCTs (334 patients) similarly found FMT was not significantly associated with weight reduction.[2] A 2025 meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (320 participants) showed a trend toward BMI reduction (MD: -0.65 kg/m²) that did not reach statistical significance (p=0.070), though sensitivity analyses suggested this finding was unstable and dependent on statistical assumptions.[3]

One notable exception: A 2025 single-arm study (not placebo-controlled) of 23 participants receiving three biweekly FMT sessions via nasojejunal tube reported that 52% achieved ≥5% weight loss at 12 weeks. Responders lost an average of 7.98 ± 2.69 kg versus 2.90 ± 1.89 kg in non-responders (P < 0.001). Responders exhibited significantly higher donor-derived microbial strain engraftment (37.8% vs 15.2%, P = 0.020), with increased abundance of Phascolarctobacterium and Acidaminococcaceae.[4]

Metabolic Effects:

FMT shows more consistent improvements in metabolic parameters than weight loss:

- Insulin sensitivity: A 2021 RCT (70 participants with severe obesity and metabolic syndrome) found that single-dose oral FMT combined with low-fermentable fiber significantly improved HOMA2-IR at 6 weeks (3.16 ± 3.01 vs 3.77 ± 3.57 at baseline, P = 0.02), while FMT with high-fermentable fiber showed no benefit.[5]

- Glycemic control: Short-term outcomes (<6 weeks) showed reductions in fasting blood glucose (MD = -0.12 mmol/L, 95% CI: -0.23, -0.01), HbA1c (MD = -0.37 mmol/mol, 95% CI: -0.73, -0.01), and insulin levels (MD = -24.77 mmol/L, 95% CI: -37.60, -11.94).[6]

- Lipids: HDL cholesterol increased (MD = 0.07 mmol/L, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.11). A 2023 meta-analysis found FMT negatively associated with total cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides.[7]

PREDIMED-Plus Findings

The PREDIMED-Plus trial examined lifestyle intervention (energy-restricted Mediterranean diet plus physical activity) rather than direct microbiota manipulation, but provides insights into diet-microbiota-weight relationships:

Weight Loss:

The intensive intervention group achieved 4.2 kg weight loss (IQR: -6.8, -2.5) at 1 year versus 0.2 kg (IQR: -2.1, 1.4) in controls (P < 0.001).[8] In the full trial (626 participants), the intervention group lost 3.2 kg versus 0.7 kg in controls (mean difference: -2.5 kg, 95% CI: -3.1 to -1.9), with 33.7% achieving ≥5% weight loss versus 11.9% in controls.[9]

Microbiota Changes:

The intervention group showed decreases in Butyricicoccus, Haemophilus, Ruminiclostridium 5, and Eubacterium hallii compared to controls.[10] Decreases in Haemophilus and Coprococcus 3 were associated with reduced adiposity in both groups. Changes in Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 positively correlated with Mediterranean diet adherence.[11]

A metabolomics substudy (400 participants) identified intervention effects on 4 stool metabolites and subnetworks involving bile acids, ceramides, sphingosines, fatty acids, and carnitines. The intervention group showed reduced abundance of Eubacterium hallii and Dorea, plus increased alpha diversity. These microbiota changes were associated with alterations in fecal metabolite profiles and cardiovascular risk factors.[12]

Baseline microbiota analysis showed enrichment of Ruminococcaceae UCG-001 and Christensenellaceae associated with greater weight loss after 12 months, while Prevotella and Dialister were enriched in participants with lower baseline BMI.[13]

Summary

Current evidence suggests gut microbiota dysbiosis is associated with but not proven to cause obesity in humans. FMT trials show minimal to no weight loss effect (effect sizes generally <1 kg or non-significant), though some metabolic parameters improve modestly. The PREDIMED-Plus findings indicate that weight loss through diet and exercise induces microbiota changes, but whether these changes mediate weight loss or simply accompany it remains unclear. Successful donor microbial engraftment may be critical for FMT efficacy, but standardized protocols and donor selection criteria are lacking.

主張二：高蛋白飲食 部分成立

「高蛋白增肌減脂、保留瘦肉組織」

High-protein diets during caloric restriction produce modest but consistent benefits for weight loss, fat mass reduction, and lean body mass preservation compared to standard-protein diets. Meta-analyses show effect sizes of approximately -0.79 kg additional weight loss, -0.87 kg greater fat mass reduction, and +0.43 kg preservation of fat-free mass with high-protein versus standard-protein energy-restricted diets.[27] However, these benefits diminish substantially at 12-24 months, with dietary adherence emerging as the primary determinant of long-term success rather than macronutrient composition.

Meta-Analysis Effect Sizes

A comprehensive meta-analysis of 24 trials (1,063 participants, mean duration 12.1 weeks) comparing isocaloric high-protein versus standard-protein low-fat diets during energy restriction found:[28]

- Body weight: -0.79 kg (95% CI: -1.50, -0.08 kg) favoring high-protein

- Fat mass: -0.87 kg (95% CI: -1.26, -0.48 kg) favoring high-protein

- Fat-free mass preservation: +0.43 kg (95% CI: 0.09, 0.78 kg) favoring high-protein

- Resting energy expenditure: +595.5 kJ/d (95% CI: 67.0, 1124.1 kJ/d) favoring high-protein

- Triglycerides: -0.23 mmol/L (95% CI: -0.33, -0.12 mmol/L) favoring high-protein

A more recent 2025 network meta-analysis of 83 studies confirmed these findings, showing standardized mean differences of -0.25 for body mass, -0.26 for BMI, and -0.19 for waist circumference with high-protein diets, along with a mean difference of -0.64 kg for fat mass and +0.34 kg for lean body mass.[29] The analysis identified that high-protein, moderate-carbohydrate, high-fat diets ranked best for reducing body mass, BMI, waist circumference, fat mass, and blood pressure while increasing lean body mass.[30]

Long-Term Outcomes at 12-24 Months

The benefits of high-protein diets attenuate significantly over longer durations. Large randomized trials and meta-analyses indicate that high-protein diets induce significantly greater weight loss during the early rapid weight loss phase (3-6 months), but differences between diets are attenuated and no longer significant during the late, slow weight loss phase (12-24 months).[18] A recent meta-analysis did show persistent benefits of higher-protein weight-loss diets on body weight and fat mass at longer durations, but the effect sizes were smaller than those observed in shorter-term studies.[19]

Gradually decreasing adherence appears responsible for this attenuation. Dietary adherence, rather than macronutrient composition, emerges as the major predictor of long-term weight loss success.[20] Improvements in weight management were detected in those who adhered to prescribed higher-protein regimens, whereas those who did not adhere had no marked improvements.[21]

DIOGENES Trial Findings

The Diet, Obesity, and Genes (DIOGENES) trial was a large pan-European randomized controlled trial examining the effects of dietary protein content and glycemic index on weight maintenance after initial weight loss. The trial enrolled 932 overweight adults across 8 European centers who first lost weight on an 8-week low-calorie diet (mean loss: 11.2 kg), then were randomized to one of five ad libitum diets for 26 weeks: high-protein/low-glycemic index (HP/LGI), high-protein/high-glycemic index (HP/HGI), low-protein/low-glycemic index (LP/LGI), low-protein/high-glycemic index (LP/HGI), or control.[22]

12-Month Results (from two centers that extended to 1 year):[23]

- Average weight regain over 12 months: 3.9 kg (95% CI: 3.0-4.8 kg)

- High-protein diets resulted in 2.0 kg less weight regain (95% CI: 0.4-3.6 kg, P=0.017) in completers analysis (N=139)

- In intention-to-treat analysis (N=256), the difference was 2.8 kg less weight regain (95% CI: 1.4-4.1 kg, P<0.001)

- No consistent effect of glycemic index on weight regain was found

- The HP/LGI diet group continued to lose weight during the 6-month intervention period despite ad libitum intake[24]

26-Week Results (full cohort):[25]

- Weight loss (-11.23 kg) reduced high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (-1.15 mg/L), lipids, and blood pressure

- During weight maintenance, low-glycemic index diets produced a -0.46 mg/L greater decrease in hs-CRP (95% CI: -0.79 to -0.13, P<0.001) compared to high-glycemic index diets

- Low-protein diets achieved a -0.25 mg/L greater reduction in hs-CRP (95% CI: -0.59 to -0.17, P<0.001) than high-protein diets

- The HP/LGI diet was associated with greater BMI loss and improved insulin resistance markers (TyG index)[26]

Gender Effects: Higher protein consumption was more effective for weight stability in both sexes, with particularly better results in obese women (low-protein: +0.73 kg vs high-protein: -0.93 kg).[27] Males showed higher weight regain overall, while women demonstrated better biochemical outcomes.[28]

Optimal Protein Intake: The evidence suggests that higher-protein diets containing 1.2-1.6 g protein/kg/day with meal-specific quantities of at least 25-30 g protein/meal provide improvements in appetite, body weight management, and cardiometabolic risk factors.[29] Higher protein intake during caloric restriction also improves overall diet quality, with increased intake of total protein and green vegetables and reduced refined grains and added sugars.[30]

主張三：進食順序 部分成立

「菜肉先、澱粉後，改善代謝」

Consuming vegetables and protein before carbohydrates reduces postprandial glucose excursions by approximately 29-40% and may modestly improve HbA1c, though evidence for weight loss is limited. A systematic review of 11 crossover trials found consistent reductions in postprandial glucose area under the curve (AUC), peak glucose concentrations, and glucose levels at specific time points when carbohydrate-rich foods were consumed last.[31]

Postprandial Glucose Effects

The most robust evidence comes from acute crossover studies demonstrating substantial glycemic benefits:

In prediabetes: A randomized crossover study (n=15) showed that consuming protein and vegetables before carbohydrates (PVF) reduced incremental glucose peaks by >40% compared to carbohydrate-first (CF) eating. The glucose incremental AUC was 38.8% lower with PVF versus CF. A vegetables-first approach (VF) showed similar glucose reductions with the lowest insulin response among all meal patterns.[32]

In type 2 diabetes: An earlier pilot study (n=11) found that consuming carbohydrates after vegetables and protein reduced postprandial glucose by 28.6% at 30 minutes (P=0.001), 36.7% at 60 minutes (P=0.001), and 16.8% at 120 minutes (P=0.03) compared to carbohydrate-first eating. Postprandial insulin levels were also reduced at 60 and 120 minutes.[33]

The systematic review noted these effects were "relatively consistent, regardless of the diabetes status of the participants," though the review characterized the isolated effects as "modest".[34]

HbA1c Effects

Direct evidence for HbA1c reduction from food order interventions is limited. The systematic review focused on acute postprandial effects rather than longer-term HbA1c outcomes.[35] However, a related meta-analysis on time-restricted eating (a different meal timing intervention) showed a mean HbA1c reduction of -0.08% (95% CI: -0.15 to -0.01) over 12-52 weeks, though this was rated as low-certainty evidence.[36]

A whey/guar preload study (n=79, 12 weeks) demonstrated sustained postprandial glucose reductions at both week 1 and week 12, with the greatest reductions at 30 and 60 minutes post-meal, suggesting potential for longer-term glycemic benefits.[37]

Weight Loss Effects

No direct evidence exists for weight loss from food order manipulation alone. The studies examining vegetable/protein-first eating controlled for total calorie intake and macronutrient composition, making weight loss an unlikely outcome. [39]

A meta-analysis examining calorie distribution (eating more calories earlier versus later in the day) found a mean weight reduction of -1.75 kg (95% CI: -2.37 to -1.13) over 12 weeks, but this was rated as low-certainty evidence and represents a different intervention than food order within meals.[40]

Mechanisms

The proposed mechanisms for glucose-lowering effects include delayed gastric emptying and slowed glucose absorption due to fiber and fat consumed before carbohydrates, though these mechanisms have not been fully elucidated.[41] The intervention also affected appetite-regulating hormones, with one study showing ghrelin levels decreased by -11.45% ± 3.86% with carbohydrate-last versus increased by 4.13% ± 4.38% with carbohydrate-first eating at 180 minutes (P=0.003).[42]

Clinical Implications

Food order represents a simple behavioral strategy that requires no calorie restriction or food elimination, potentially improving adherence compared to more restrictive dietary approaches.[43] The Obesity Society's 2025 clinical guidelines note that "timing the carbohydrate ingestion can lead to improvements in glycemia and the associated hormone excursions".[44] However, the systematic review emphasized that "further research on the effectiveness of dietary strategies that incorporate ordered/sequential eating to establish any longer-term effects under free living conditions is warranted".[45]

主張四：重設代謝定點 與證據牴觸

「重新編碼定點，從此不復胖」

Adaptive thermogenesis (metabolic adaptation) occurs after weight loss and persists during weight maintenance, but its magnitude is modest and influenced by energy balance status. No diet has been shown to permanently reset the metabolic set point. Evidence suggests that metabolic adaptation is attenuated or absent during neutral energy balance, and both GLP-1 agonists and bariatric surgery can overcome these adaptations through distinct mechanisms.

Metabolic Adaptation and Adaptive Thermogenesis

Adaptive thermogenesis refers to the mass-independent decrease in energy expenditure beyond what would be predicted from changes in body composition alone.[61] This phenomenon manifests in both resting energy expenditure (REE) and non-resting energy expenditure, appearing throughout all periods of weight loss and persisting during subsequent weight maintenance.[62]

The timeline and magnitude of these changes are clinically relevant. REE decreases significantly at approximately 5% weight loss (occurring around day 12), while exercise-induced energy expenditure falls at 10% weight loss (around day 32).[63] At 10% weight loss, transient adaptive thermogenesis of approximately -460 kJ/day (-110 kcal/day) has been observed.[64] After diet-induced weight loss of 14 kg, metabolic adaptation averaged -92 kcal/day during active weight loss, which decreased to -38 kcal/day after weight stabilization.[65]

Energy Balance and Metabolic Adaptation

A critical finding is that metabolic adaptation is dependent on energy balance status.[66] When participants are in negative energy balance (actively losing weight), metabolic adaptation is present. However, this adaptation is reduced by approximately half after a period of weight stabilization, and in some studies disappears entirely when participants achieve neutral energy balance.[67] In one study, metabolic adaptation was present at weeks 9 and 13 post-weight loss but was no longer detectable at 1-year follow-up.[68]

Well-designed studies with weight stabilization phases report lower or non-significant values for adaptive thermogenesis compared to studies measuring during active weight loss.[69] This suggests that the magnitude of metabolic adaptation may be smaller than previously thought when measured under appropriate conditions.

Can Diet Reset the Metabolic Set Point?

Current evidence indicates that no dietary intervention permanently resets the metabolic set point. Adaptive thermogenesis persists for at least one year when weight loss is maintained, with the ratio of measured to predicted RMR remaining significantly reduced at 52 weeks post-weight loss.[70] The correlation between weight loss magnitude and metabolic adaptation persists throughout the weight maintenance period, suggesting an ongoing physiological defense of the previous higher body weight.[71]

Leptin and Weight Regain Physiology

While the search results did not provide extensive detail on leptin-specific changes, the broader physiological adaptations that promote weight regain are well-documented. Importantly, metabolic adaptation at the level of RMR does not predict weight regain at 1-year follow-up, suggesting that other factors (hormonal changes, appetite regulation, behavioral factors) may be more important drivers of weight regain than metabolic adaptation alone.[72]

GLP-1 Agonist Evidence

GLP-1 receptor agonists demonstrate efficacy in overcoming weight regain after both dietary weight loss and bariatric surgery. In patients who experienced insufficient weight loss or weight regain following metabolic/bariatric surgery, GLP-1 agonists produced moderate additional weight loss with a Cohen's d-score of 0.470.[73] Meta-analysis shows that liraglutide and semaglutide induce weight reduction of 7.83 kg compared to pre-treatment weight in post-bariatric patients, with 23% reporting adverse events but only 7% discontinuing treatment.[74]

In randomized controlled trials, GLP-1 agonists produced 4.36 kg greater weight loss compared to placebo in post-bariatric patients.[75] These agents work through appetite suppression, slowing gastric emptying, and promoting fat loss, potentially offering a pharmacologic approach to counteract the physiological adaptations that promote weight regain.[76]

When comparing GLP-1 agonists to bariatric surgery in treatment-naive patients, metabolic/bariatric surgery achieves superior outcomes. At less than 104 weeks, surgery produced 10.3% greater total weight loss, 4.5 kg/m² greater BMI reduction, and 11.7 kg greater absolute weight loss compared to GLP-1 agonists.[77] These differences persisted at ≥104 weeks for total weight loss (-9.1%) and body weight (-14.6 kg).[78] However, when comparing tirzepatide (a dual GLP-1/GIP agonist) specifically to surgery, differences were not statistically significant.[79]

Bariatric Surgery Evidence

Bariatric procedures, particularly vertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG) and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), are considered metabolic surgeries because they affect metabolism in a weight loss-independent manner.[80] Improvements in glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity can occur before substantial postoperative weight loss.[81]

A key mechanism appears to be the sharp postprandial rise in endogenous GLP-1 and other gut peptides following VSG and RYGB.[82] This increase in endogenous GLP-1 signaling is considered a primary pathway leading to postsurgical weight loss and improvements in glucose metabolism, suggesting that bariatric surgery may partially overcome metabolic adaptation through enhanced incretin responses.[83]

For patients with weight regain or insufficient weight loss after bariatric surgery, GLP-1 agonists can provide additional benefit, potentially reducing the need for revisional procedures.[84] However, appetite suppression from GLP-1 agonists may worsen inadequate protein intake and micronutrient deficiencies in post-bariatric patients, requiring structured monitoring and supplementation.[85]

Clinical Implications

The evidence indicates that metabolic adaptation is a real but modest phenomenon that contributes to the difficulty of maintaining weight loss. While no dietary intervention permanently resets the metabolic set point, both pharmacologic (GLP-1 agonists) and surgical interventions can overcome these adaptations through mechanisms beyond simple caloric restriction. The magnitude of metabolic adaptation (approximately 40-90 kcal/day) is clinically significant over time but represents only a portion of the physiological changes that promote weight regain.

主張五：安全性 有條件風險

「安全、人人都能長期執行」

High-protein very low calorie diets (VLCDs) pose specific risks for CKD patients and carry increased gallstone risk during rapid weight loss. The KDIGO 2024 guidelines explicitly recommend avoiding high protein intake (>1.3 g/kg/d) in adults with CKD at risk of progression.[46] For CKD patients, the recommended protein intake is 0.8 g/kg/d, with very low-protein diets (0.3-0.4 g/kg/d with supplementation) reserved only for select patients under close supervision.[47]

KDIGO 2024 Protein Recommendations for CKD

The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines provide clear practice points:[48]

- Avoid protein intake >1.3 g/kg/d in adults with CKD at risk of progression

- Maintain 0.8 g/kg/d for adults with CKD G3-G5 (the WHO recommended dietary allowance)

- Very low-protein diets (0.3-0.4 g/kg/d supplemented with essential amino acids or ketoacid analogs) may be considered only in willing, able patients at risk of kidney failure under close supervision

- Do not prescribe low- or very low-protein diets in metabolically unstable CKD patients

The rationale is that excess dietary protein cannot be stored and leads to accumulation of uremic toxins, increased intraglomerular pressure, and glomerular hyperfiltration, potentially accelerating kidney damage.[49] Observational evidence suggests excess protein intake may accelerate kidney functional decline.[50] The guidelines emphasize balancing potential benefits against risks of malnutrition, sarcopenia, and cachexia.[51]

Gallstone Risk During Rapid Weight Loss

VLCDs significantly increase gallstone formation risk. The prevalence of new gallstones reaches 10-12% after 8-16 weeks of low-calorie diets and >30% within 12-18 months after gastric bypass surgery.[52] Approximately one-third of these stones become symptomatic.[53]

A matched cohort study of 6,642 participants compared VLCD (500 kcal/d) versus LCD (1200-1500 kcal/d) over 1 year.[54] The VLCD group experienced:

- 3.4-fold higher risk of symptomatic gallstones requiring hospitalization (hazard ratio 3.4, 95% CI 1.8-6.3)

- Number needed to harm (NNH) for symptomatic gallstones: 92 (95% CI 63-168)

- NNH for cholecystectomy: 151 (95% CI 94-377)

- 61% of gallstone events resulted in cholecystectomy

Risk factors for gallstone formation during weight loss include:[55]

- Relative weight loss >24% of initial body weight

- Weight loss rate >1.5 kg/week

- Very low-calorie diet with no fat content

- Long overnight fasting periods

- High serum triglyceride levels

The mechanism involves gallbladder stasis from reduced stimulation by low-fat diets, with at least 10 g of fat needed for efficient gallbladder emptying.[56] VLCDs also increase bile cholesterol saturation and mucin secretion.[57]

Micronutrient Deficiency Risk

While the search results did not provide specific data on micronutrient deficiencies with VLCDs, the KDIGO guidelines emphasize concerns about nutritional deficiencies with very low-protein diets, particularly in elderly or highly comorbid populations.[58] The guidelines stress that low-protein diets should only be implemented under close supervision with adequate energy intake and consistent monitoring.[59]

Clinical Implications

For CKD patients considering weight loss, high-protein VLCDs are contraindicated. Standard protein intake should be maintained at 0.8 g/kg/d, avoiding excess (>1.3 g/kg/d). When rapid weight loss is pursued, the substantial gallstone risk (NNH 92 for symptomatic stones) should be discussed, and strategies to mitigate risk—such as ensuring adequate dietary fat content or considering ursodeoxycholic acid prophylaxis—may be warranted.[60]

主張六：優越性 未證實

「優於一般飲食或單純少吃」

No single diet is superior for long-term weight loss—network meta-analyses and systematic reviews consistently show that macronutrient composition matters far less than dietary adherence, with most differences between diets being small and clinically unimportant beyond 12 months.[86]

BMJ Network Meta-Analysis Findings

The 2020 BMJ network meta-analysis of 121 trials (21,942 patients) comparing 14 named diets found that at 6 months, low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets produced similar weight loss (4.63 kg vs 4.37 kg, both moderate certainty) compared to usual diet.[87] Among named diets, Atkins (5.5 kg), DASH (3.6 kg), and Zone (4.1 kg) showed the largest effects at 6 months. However, weight loss diminished substantially at 12 months across all macronutrient patterns and named diets, with cardiovascular benefits essentially disappearing for all interventions except the Mediterranean diet.[88]

A 2014 JAMA network meta-analysis of 48 trials similarly found that while statistical differences existed among diets, the differences were small and unlikely to be important to those seeking weight loss. For example, Atkins resulted in only 1.71 kg more weight loss than Zone at 6 months.[89] Both low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets achieved approximately 8 kg weight loss at 6 months, with 1-2 kg regained by 12 months.[90]

POUNDS Lost Trial and Adherence

The POUNDS Lost trial (Preventing Overweight Using Novel Dietary Strategies) directly tested whether macronutrient composition affects weight loss by comparing four diets with different fat and protein compositions, all with identical caloric restriction. A secondary analysis of this trial demonstrated that the combination of high dietary quality and high adherence produced significantly greater BMI reduction than either factor alone.[91] Participants who achieved both high dietary quality (above median HEI-2010 score improvement) and high adherence (greater macronutrient target achievement) lost 1.15 kg/m² more BMI in the low-carbohydrate group and 1.11 kg/m² more in the low-fat group compared to those with low quality and low adherence.[92]

Cochrane Review Perspective

The Cochrane systematic review on low-carbohydrate versus balanced-carbohydrate diets emphasizes that adherence to diets is a primary driver of weight-loss success, regardless of macronutrient composition, and may explain most of whether dieters achieve energy deficit for weight loss.[95] The review notes that dietary adherence varies widely and that maintaining weight loss over the long term remains a considerable challenge, with most people tending to relapse.[96]

Role of Adherence vs Macronutrient Composition

Multiple lines of evidence converge on the conclusion that caloric restriction is the common pathway for weight reduction, but different diets may facilitate weight loss primarily by enhancing dietary adherence.[97] A narrative review of meta-analyses found few significant long-term differences in weight loss for diets of varying macronutrient composition, although some regimens showed short-term advantages (e.g., low-carbohydrate versus low-fat in the first 6 months).[98]

Recent analyses suggest that glycemic load reduction may mediate much of the weight loss effect attributed to various diets. A reanalysis of the DIETFITS trial found that measures related to carbohydrate intake showed strong associations with weight loss, whereas those related to total fat intake showed weak to no associations, with glycemic load explaining most of the observed effect of calorie intake on weight change.[99]

Clinical Implications

International guidelines increasingly recognize that the ideal diet is the one best adhered to by individuals.[100] The Canadian Adult Obesity Clinical Practice Guideline notes that while caloric restriction achieves short-term weight reduction (up to 12 months), there is no proven sustainable long-term weight loss effect exceeding 12 months.[101] The American Heart Association, Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, and German Obesity Society guidelines state that macronutrient composition is insignificant as long as the diet is balanced and healthy.[102]

For clinical practice, this evidence suggests focusing on individualized dietary plans based on patient preferences and cultural factors, emphasizing both dietary quality and adherence support, rather than prescribing specific macronutrient ratios.
